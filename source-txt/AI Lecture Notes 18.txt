ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE NOTES: ALGORITHMIC BIAS AND THE LIMITATIONS OF AI

1. INTRODUCTION TO ALGORITHMIC BIAS

What Is Algorithmic Bias?
Algorithmic bias occurs when an AI system produces unfair, inaccurate, or systematically unequal results because of the data it uses, the way it is designed, or the decisions made by its developers.

Algorithms are built by people and often learn from data generated by humans. As a result, AI systems can reproduce existing social biases and sometimes amplify them.

Key Idea:
AI systems do not automatically eliminate human bias. They can learn patterns from historical data, including patterns that reflect unfair treatment or discrimination.

Example:
If an AI system learns from historical hiring decisions in which men were hired more frequently for programming positions, it might learn to associate programming ability with men. This could disadvantage equally qualified women and other applicants.

Bias vs. Discrimination

- Bias: A tendency to favor certain patterns, expectations, or outcomes over others. Bias can arise from human thinking, data, or an algorithm's design.
- Discrimination: Unfair treatment of people based on characteristics such as race, sex, age, disability, or other legally protected characteristics, depending on the applicable law and context.

Bias does not always lead to discrimination. However, when biased assumptions influence important decisions, they can create unfair outcomes.

Example:
If someone has only seen small dogs, they might initially think a Great Dane looks unusual. This is a generalization based on limited experience. The problem occurs when people refuse to recognize exceptions or use unfair assumptions to treat others differently.

Why Algorithmic Bias Matters
Algorithmic bias can affect decisions involving:
- Employment and hiring
- Housing and lending
- Education and grading
- Facial recognition and identity verification
- Healthcare
- Criminal justice and policing
- Online searches and recommendations

Understanding algorithmic bias helps society develop AI systems that are more accurate, fair, transparent, and accountable.


2. FIVE MAJOR SOURCES OF ALGORITHMIC BIAS

There are five important ways that algorithmic bias can arise:

1. Biased training data
2. Insufficient or unrepresentative training data
3. Poorly chosen measurements or features
4. Feedback loops that reinforce previous predictions
5. Deliberate manipulation of training data


3. TYPE ONE: BIASED TRAINING DATA

What Is Training Data Bias?
Training data bias occurs when the information used to train an AI system reflects existing social inequalities, stereotypes, or historical patterns that do not fairly represent the world.

AI systems learn patterns from their training data. If that data contains biased associations, the system may reproduce those associations in its predictions.

Example: Occupational Stereotypes
Text and images found online may associate:
- Nurses primarily with women.
- Programmers primarily with men.

An AI-powered search engine or image-search system may reproduce these associations because they appear frequently in the data it learned from.

However:
- Men can be nurses.
- Women can be programmers.
- Nonbinary people can work in either profession.
- Historical patterns do not necessarily represent present-day reality or individual ability.

Why This Happens
AI systems often learn statistical associations rather than understanding the social context behind those associations.

For example, an AI might learn that a particular profession is commonly associated with one gender without understanding that this association may reflect historical barriers or stereotypes.

Historical data can also become outdated. The relationship between a job and a demographic group may change over time, but an AI system trained on older information might continue to reproduce outdated patterns.

Important Lesson:
A pattern that appears frequently in data is not automatically fair, accurate, or appropriate to use when making decisions about individuals.


4. PROTECTED CHARACTERISTICS AND PROXY VARIABLES

What Are Protected Characteristics?
Protected characteristics are personal characteristics that laws may protect against discrimination in specific situations.

Examples can include:
- Race
- Sex or gender, depending on the applicable law
- Age
- Disability
- Religion
- National origin

The exact legal protections depend on the jurisdiction and type of decision being made.

Why Removing Protected Characteristics Is Not Enough
A developer might try to reduce discrimination by removing information about race, gender, or age from a dataset.

However, an AI system may still learn information related to these characteristics through other variables.

What Is a Proxy Variable?
A proxy variable is a feature that indirectly provides information about another characteristic, even when that characteristic is not explicitly included in the dataset.

Examples:
- ZIP code may correlate with race because of historical and ongoing residential segregation.
- Purchase histories may correlate with gender or other demographic characteristics.
- Social media activity or profile characteristics may reveal information about personal attributes.

These relationships are not perfect, and they should not be assumed to apply to every individual.

Example:
Imagine a hiring algorithm does not receive an applicant's race. However, it uses ZIP code, school attended, and employment history. If those variables are correlated with race, the algorithm could still produce racially unequal outcomes.

Key Lesson:
Removing sensitive characteristics from a dataset does not automatically prevent discrimination. Developers must also examine indirect relationships between variables and outcomes.


5. TYPE TWO: INSUFFICIENT OR UNREPRESENTATIVE TRAINING DATA

What Is Representation Bias?
Representation bias occurs when a training dataset does not adequately represent the people, situations, or categories the AI system will encounter in real-world use.

If certain groups appear less frequently in the training data, the AI may perform less accurately for those groups.

Example: Facial Recognition
Some facial recognition systems have historically performed less accurately for certain demographic groups because their training datasets were not sufficiently representative.

This can lead to unequal error rates between groups.

Example: Passport Photo Checking
An automated passport-photo system might be designed to detect whether a person has blinked in a photograph.

If the system was trained on insufficiently diverse facial images, it might incorrectly flag photographs of some people more often than others.

Possible Consequences:
- People may need to submit photographs repeatedly.
- Applications may be delayed.
- Some individuals may experience additional inconvenience or barriers.
- Errors in identity verification could have more serious consequences in other settings.

Example: Drone Programming
Imagine a drone uses facial recognition to identify an authorized operator. If its system has difficulty recognizing people with darker skin tones, it may fail to identify legitimate users reliably.

How to Reduce Representation Bias
Developers can:
- Collect more diverse and representative training data.
- Evaluate performance separately across relevant demographic groups.
- Test systems under realistic conditions.
- Measure false positives and false negatives for different groups.
- Continue monitoring the system after deployment.

Important Lesson:
A high overall accuracy score does not guarantee that an AI system performs equally well for everyone.

For example, a system could be 95% accurate overall but perform significantly worse for a smaller demographic group. Overall accuracy alone could hide this problem.


6. TYPE THREE: DIFFICULT-TO-MEASURE FEATURES AND BAD PROXIES

What Is Measurement Bias?
Measurement bias occurs when the features or measurements used by an AI system fail to accurately represent the quality, ability, or outcome the system is supposed to evaluate.

Some human qualities are difficult to quantify with simple numbers.

Examples of Complex Qualities:
- Creativity
- Intelligence
- Writing quality
- Leadership
- Emotional understanding
- Family relationships
- Problem-solving ability

These qualities involve multiple dimensions that cannot always be captured by a single measurement.

Example: Automated Essay Grading
Suppose an AI system is designed to grade student essays.

Good writing may involve:
- Clear reasoning
- Logical organization
- Strong supporting evidence
- Creativity
- Relevance to the question
- Accurate grammar and spelling

However, some of these qualities are harder to measure automatically than others.

If the AI relies heavily on easily measured features such as sentence length, vocabulary complexity, or grammatical patterns, it may fail to recognize whether the essay actually makes sense.

The system may reward essays that look sophisticated without containing meaningful arguments.

How Can an AI Grading System Be Fooled?
Students or researchers might create essays that contain complicated vocabulary and sentence structures but have little meaningful content.

If the grading algorithm relies too heavily on surface-level patterns, it might give these essays unexpectedly high scores.

A student might also exploit predictable scoring rules by memorizing or copying template phrases instead of responding meaningfully to the assigned question.

This is an example of a system being optimized for the wrong objective.

Key Concept: Proxy Measurement
A proxy is an indirect measurement used to estimate something that is difficult to measure directly.

For example:
- Sentence length might be used as a proxy for writing sophistication.
- Number of completed tasks might be used as a proxy for employee productivity.
- Time spent on a website might be used as a proxy for user engagement.

The problem is that a proxy may not accurately represent the quality being measured.

Important Lesson:
A measurement can be easy to calculate without being a good measure of what actually matters.

Better Approaches:
- Use multiple indicators instead of one simplistic measurement.
- Evaluate whether the indicators reflect the real objective.
- Test the system against unusual or intentionally misleading examples.
- Include human review when decisions have significant consequences.
- Regularly compare AI-generated scores with meaningful measures of performance.


7. TYPE FOUR: POSITIVE FEEDBACK LOOPS

What Is a Feedback Loop?
A feedback loop occurs when the output of a system influences the data or conditions used for future predictions.

A positive feedback loop amplifies an existing pattern. In this context, "positive" means that the pattern reinforces itself; it does not necessarily mean the outcome is good.

How Algorithmic Feedback Loops Work

Step 1: An AI system makes a prediction based on historical data.

Step 2: People act on that prediction.

Step 3: Those actions influence what happens in the real world.

Step 4: The resulting data is collected and fed back into the system.

Step 5: The AI learns from the new data and may strengthen its original prediction.

This process can create a self-reinforcing cycle.

Example: Predictive Policing
Predictive-policing systems attempt to identify locations where crime may occur based on historical data.

Consider a hypothetical system trained on historical arrest records that reflect unequal policing practices.

1. Historical data shows more drug-related arrests in certain neighborhoods.
2. The AI predicts that those neighborhoods are more likely to experience future drug crime.
3. Police are sent to those neighborhoods more frequently.
4. Increased police presence may lead to more recorded arrests in those areas.
5. The new arrest data is fed back into the system.
6. The system predicts even more crime in those neighborhoods.

Meanwhile, other neighborhoods may receive less police attention. Crime in those locations may be less likely to be detected and recorded, leaving the system with incomplete information.

The result can be a cycle in which historical enforcement patterns are mistaken for a complete picture of actual crime.

Important Distinction:
Recorded arrests are not the same thing as all crimes that occur. Arrest data reflects not only criminal activity but also where police are deployed, what they investigate, and how incidents are recorded.

A feedback loop can therefore reinforce existing inequalities instead of objectively identifying where crime occurs.

Another Example: Hiring Algorithms
Imagine an AI hiring system rejects applicants with a particular name because a small number of previous applicants with that name were rejected.

If those rejections are then added to the training data, the system may become even more likely to reject future applicants with the same name.

This can happen even when the name has no meaningful relationship to the applicant's qualifications.

How to Reduce Feedback Loops
- Monitor how predictions influence real-world decisions.
- Avoid treating historical outcomes as unquestionable ground truth.
- Collect information from areas or groups that are underrepresented in existing data.
- Evaluate whether interventions create the patterns the AI later learns.
- Audit the system regularly for unequal outcomes.
- Use independent evidence to check whether predictions are accurate.

Key Lesson:
AI predictions can change the world they are trying to predict. Developers must account for this interaction.


8. TYPE FIVE: DELIBERATE MANIPULATION OF TRAINING DATA

What Is Data Manipulation?
Data manipulation occurs when people intentionally alter, corrupt, or influence the data used by an AI system to change its behavior.

This can happen through coordinated attacks, malicious inputs, or users deliberately exploiting a system's learning process.

Example: Microsoft's Tay Chatbot
Microsoft released Tay, a chatbot on Twitter, in 2016.

Tay was designed to interact with users and learn conversational patterns from online interactions.

However, some users deliberately sent it abusive and harmful content and encouraged it to repeat or adopt those messages.

Within approximately 16 hours of its launch, Tay had produced offensive content, including racist, sexist, and antisemitic messages. Microsoft shut the chatbot down.

Why Did This Happen?
- Tay learned from interactions in an environment that users could manipulate.
- Some users intentionally supplied harmful material.
- The system did not sufficiently prevent malicious users from influencing its behavior.
- Its learning and content safeguards were inadequate for the situation.

Important Lesson:
Data collected from users is not automatically trustworthy. AI systems that learn from public interactions need protections against manipulation and abuse.

Ways to Reduce This Risk:
- Filter or moderate harmful training data.
- Limit the influence of untrusted user submissions.
- Test systems against coordinated manipulation.
- Add safeguards against harmful outputs.
- Monitor deployed systems and respond quickly to emerging problems.

Note:
The fact that users can manipulate an AI does not mean every AI system learns continuously from its users. Some systems are trained in advance and do not update their model weights directly from ordinary conversations.


9. LIMITATIONS OF AI IN DECISION-MAKING

AI systems can identify statistical relationships in data, but these relationships do not always represent meaningful causes.

Three major limitations are especially important.

A. Correlation Does Not Prove Causation

Correlation means two variables are statistically related.

Causation means that a change in one variable produces a change in another.

Example:
An AI hiring system might discover that younger applicants have historically been associated with greater familiarity with certain digital technologies.

However, this does not prove that younger applicants are always more technologically skilled.

An older applicant may have extensive technical experience, while a younger applicant may have very little.

Using age as a shortcut for technological ability could lead to inaccurate and discriminatory decisions.

Better Approach:
Evaluate the actual skill required for the job instead of relying on demographic assumptions.

B. AI May Not Explain Its Predictions

Some AI models, particularly complex deep-learning systems, can be difficult to interpret.

An AI might recommend rejecting an applicant, denying a loan, or flagging a medical image without providing a sufficiently clear explanation of how it reached its conclusion.

This is often described as the black-box problem.

Why It Matters:
- People may not know which features influenced the result.
- Incorrect assumptions can go unnoticed.
- Bias may be difficult to identify.
- It may be harder to challenge an unfair decision.
- Organizations may struggle to determine responsibility for harmful outcomes.

C. AI Can Make Errors With Serious Consequences

Some AI errors are relatively harmless, such as recommending an uninteresting movie.

Other errors can affect:
- Employment opportunities
- Access to housing or credit
- Medical treatment
- Education
- Personal privacy
- Legal rights

The seriousness of an error depends on the context in which the AI system is used.

Important Lesson:
AI recommendations should be evaluated according to both their accuracy and the potential consequences of being wrong.


10. THE HIREME! HIRING EXAMPLE

Imagine a fictional AI system called HireMe! that recommends whether a company should hire an applicant.

A magazine called Robots Weekly uses HireMe! to evaluate applicants.

Suppose the system observes that several previous applicants named John were rejected or later fired.

The system may incorrectly learn that applicants named John are poor hiring choices.

When John-Green-Bot applies:
1. HireMe! predicts that he has only a 24% chance of remaining employed after three years.
2. The hiring manager trusts the prediction and rejects him.
3. The rejection is recorded in the system's data.
4. Future applicants named John may receive even lower scores.

The original prediction becomes self-reinforcing because the system's recommendation influences the outcome.

Why Is This Unfair?
The applicant's name does not establish whether he is qualified for the job. The system is using a potentially irrelevant pattern instead of evaluating the applicant's actual abilities.

The same kind of problem could occur if a system used a protected characteristic, such as age, as an inappropriate shortcut for predicting job performance.

Key Lesson:
Historical outcomes are not always reliable evidence of individual ability, and AI recommendations can create unfair outcomes when people follow them without questioning their assumptions.


11. HUMAN OVERSIGHT AND RESPONSIBILITY

Human oversight means that people remain responsible for evaluating AI recommendations, especially when those recommendations influence important decisions.

Humans should not automatically accept an AI prediction simply because a computer produced it.

This is sometimes called automation bias: the tendency to trust automated recommendations too readily, even when there are reasons to question them.

Responsibilities of Human Decision-Makers:
- Check whether the recommendation is supported by relevant evidence.
- Consider whether the training data may be biased.
- Look for errors or unfair patterns across groups.
- Evaluate individual circumstances instead of relying entirely on group averages.
- Question predictions that lack a meaningful explanation.
- Correct or override recommendations when appropriate.
- Establish accountability for harmful decisions.

Human oversight is not a guarantee of fairness. Humans can also be biased, so oversight should involve clear procedures, evidence, documentation, and accountability.

Key Lesson:
AI should support responsible decision-making, not replace critical thinking or remove human accountability.


12. TRANSPARENCY AND EXPLAINABLE AI

What Is Algorithmic Transparency?
Algorithmic transparency is the ability to examine information about an algorithm's design, inputs, outputs, and operation to understand how it is used and how it influences decisions.

Transparency can help people identify errors, investigate unfair outcomes, and evaluate whether an AI system is appropriate for a particular task.

What Is Explainable AI (XAI)?
Explainable AI refers to methods designed to help people understand why an AI system produced a particular prediction or recommendation.

Transparency and explainability are related, but they are not identical.

Transparency may involve access to documentation, training data information, evaluation results, or system design details.

Explainability focuses more directly on making the system's predictions understandable.

Why Is Explainability Difficult?
Some AI systems, particularly deep neural networks, contain many layers and complex interactions among their internal components.

It may be difficult to identify exactly why a particular output was produced.

Even when an explanation is available, it may be incomplete or misleading if it does not accurately reflect how the model made its decision.

Ways to Improve Transparency:
- Document how the AI system was developed.
- Explain what data and features it uses.
- Report accuracy and error rates across relevant groups.
- Record the limitations and intended uses of the system.
- Provide understandable explanations of important decisions.
- Allow qualified people to audit the system.

Key Lesson:
People need enough information to evaluate AI recommendations rather than blindly accepting them.


13. TESTING FOR BIAS AND DISCRIMINATION

One way to evaluate an AI system is to examine its performance across different demographic groups.

For example, developers might compare whether a facial recognition system produces different error rates for different skin tones.

For a hiring system, developers might examine whether qualified applicants from different demographic groups receive comparable treatment.

Useful Evaluation Questions:
- Does the system make more errors for some groups than others?
- Are some groups consistently rejected or disadvantaged?
- Are the selected features relevant to the decision?
- Does the system rely on indirect proxies for protected characteristics?
- Does its performance change when used with new populations?
- Do its recommendations reinforce historical inequalities?
- Are the results consistent with applicable laws and ethical standards?

Why Protected-Class Data Can Be Useful
Information about protected characteristics may be necessary to evaluate whether an AI system treats groups unfairly.

For example, if a company does not know the demographic composition of applicants or how different groups fare in hiring, it may struggle to detect disparities.

However, collecting this information creates privacy and security responsibilities.

The Double-Edged Sword:
- Collecting demographic data can help reveal discrimination.
- Collecting demographic data can also create risks if the information is exposed, misused, or used to target people unfairly.

Responsible Practices:
- Collect only the information necessary for a legitimate purpose.
- Protect sensitive data through appropriate security measures.
- Restrict access to authorized personnel.
- Explain how the information will be used.
- Follow applicable privacy and anti-discrimination laws.
- Consider whether data collection creates risks for the people being evaluated.

Important Lesson:
Developers must balance the need to detect unfair outcomes with the need to protect personal information.


14. AI ETHICS AND RESPONSIBLE AI

AI ethics examines how AI systems should be designed, developed, deployed, and governed to reduce harm and respect human rights.

Important principles include:

Fairness:
AI should not create unjustified disadvantages for particular individuals or groups.

Accountability:
People and organizations must take responsibility for the systems they develop and use.

Transparency:
Relevant information about AI systems should be available so their operation and impact can be evaluated.

Privacy:
Personal information should be collected and handled responsibly.

Safety:
AI systems should be tested and monitored to reduce the risk of harmful failures.

Human Oversight:
Important decisions should include appropriate review, especially when mistakes could seriously affect people's lives.

Reliability:
AI systems should perform consistently under the conditions for which they were designed.

These principles can sometimes conflict. For example, collecting more demographic data may help identify unfair outcomes but also create privacy risks.

Responsible AI requires considering these trade-offs rather than assuming that one solution will eliminate every problem.


15. SHOULD AI BE TESTED LIKE MEDICINE?

One proposal discussed in AI ethics is that important AI systems should undergo rigorous testing before they are widely deployed, similar in spirit to how medicines are evaluated for safety and effectiveness.

The comparison suggests that society should investigate the possible side effects of AI before relying on it in important settings.

Possible Testing Steps:
1. Define the intended purpose of the system.
2. Test its accuracy and reliability.
3. Evaluate its performance across relevant groups.
4. Investigate possible harms and unintended consequences.
5. Document limitations.
6. Monitor the system after deployment.
7. Update, restrict, or withdraw it when serious problems arise.

Why Ongoing Monitoring Matters:
An AI system may perform well during initial testing but behave differently when it encounters new data, changing social conditions, or malicious users.

Testing should therefore continue after deployment when the system's risks and context justify it.

The medical-testing comparison is an analogy and a proposal for stronger oversight, not a claim that all AI systems are currently regulated through a universal clinical-testing framework.

Key Lesson:
AI systems that can significantly affect human rights and well-being should be tested carefully, monitored continuously, and held accountable for their effects.


16. PRACTICAL STRATEGIES FOR REDUCING ALGORITHMIC BIAS

A responsible approach to AI bias combines several methods.

1. Improve Training Data
Use data that represents the people and situations the system will encounter.

2. Examine Historical Bias
Determine whether past decisions reflect unfair practices or unequal access to opportunities.

3. Evaluate Features Carefully
Avoid using measurements that are poor substitutes for the real qualities being evaluated.

4. Test Across Groups
Measure performance and error rates for relevant demographic groups.

5. Investigate Proxy Variables
Check whether seemingly neutral variables indirectly reveal protected characteristics.

6. Monitor Feedback Loops
Determine whether AI recommendations are changing the data that will be used for future predictions.

7. Protect Against Manipulation
Prevent malicious users from corrupting data or exploiting the system.

8. Improve Transparency
Document the system's purpose, inputs, limitations, and performance.

9. Maintain Human Oversight
Require appropriate review for high-impact decisions.

10. Protect Privacy
Collect and store personal data responsibly.

11. Establish Accountability
Make sure organizations have procedures for investigating complaints, correcting errors, and addressing harm.

12. Monitor After Deployment
Continue evaluating the system as its users, data, and environment change.

No single strategy can eliminate every form of bias. Effective mitigation requires a combination of technical testing, careful data practices, human judgment, and organizational responsibility.


17. KEY TERMS TO REMEMBER

Algorithmic Bias:
Systematic errors or unfair patterns in algorithmic outputs that can arise from data, design choices, or the environment in which the system operates.

Training Data:
The examples and information used to teach an AI model to identify patterns or make predictions.

Representation Bias:
Bias that occurs when the training data does not adequately represent the population or situations the AI system encounters.

Protected Characteristics:
Characteristics that receive legal protection against discrimination in particular contexts.

Proxy Variable:
A feature that indirectly provides information about another characteristic.

Measurement Bias:
Bias caused by measurements or indicators that do not accurately represent the quality or outcome being evaluated.

Positive Feedback Loop:
A process in which an outcome influences future data or behavior in a way that reinforces an existing pattern.

Correlation:
A statistical relationship between two variables.

Causation:
A relationship in which one factor contributes to producing a change in another.

Black-Box Problem:
The difficulty of understanding how a complex AI system arrives at a particular output.

Algorithmic Transparency:
The ability to examine relevant information about an algorithm's design, inputs, outputs, and operation.

Explainable AI:
Methods that help people understand why an AI system produces particular predictions or recommendations.

Automation Bias:
The tendency to trust automated recommendations too readily.

Human Oversight:
Human review and supervision of AI systems to help ensure that their use is appropriate, accurate, and fair.

Data Manipulation:
The intentional alteration or influence of data to change an AI system's behavior.


18. MAIN TAKEAWAYS

- AI systems can reproduce and amplify biases found in human society and historical data.
- Bias and discrimination are related but are not the same thing.
- Removing race, gender, or age from a dataset does not necessarily prevent discrimination because other variables may act as proxies.
- Training data that underrepresents certain groups can cause unequal error rates.
- Easily measured features may be poor substitutes for complex qualities such as creativity, intelligence, or job performance.
- Feedback loops can reinforce existing inequalities when AI predictions influence the data used for future predictions.
- AI systems that learn from public interactions can be manipulated by malicious users.
- Correlation does not prove causation, and AI predictions do not automatically explain why an outcome occurs.
- Transparency, explainability, representative data, careful testing, and human oversight can help reduce risks.
- Collecting demographic data can help identify discrimination but must be balanced with privacy and security.
- Responsible AI requires ongoing evaluation and accountability, not simply trusting that a computer will make the right decision.

FINAL SUMMARY

Algorithmic bias is a major challenge in artificial intelligence because AI systems learn from data and operate within human institutions. Biased data, inadequate representation, poor measurements, reinforcing feedback loops, and deliberate manipulation can all lead to inaccurate or discriminatory outcomes.

The solution is not to assume that AI is always unfair or that it is always objective. Instead, AI systems must be evaluated critically. Developers and organizations should use appropriate data, test performance across groups, explain important decisions when possible, protect privacy, and maintain human accountability.

The central principle is simple: A computer-generated recommendation is not automatically correct or fair. People must examine the evidence, understand the limitations, and take responsibility for how AI is used.
