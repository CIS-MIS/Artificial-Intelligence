6/8. Independence
3◼Some variables are inherently unrelated
❑In P(Toothache,Catch,Cavity,Weather), weather does
not affect nor is affected by other variables
◼The notion is captured byindependence
❑P(A,B) = P(A)P(B) : A and B areindependent
◼Example
❑Flipping 2 coins at once, is itindependent?
❑P(c1=H,c2=H) = P(c1=H,c2=T) = P(c1=T,c2=H) =  
P(c1=T,c2=T) =0.25
◼More than two variables: P(A,B,C) = P(A)P(B)P(C)
Independence
4◼Independence can be expressed in other equivalent  
ways
❑Remember P(A,B) = P(A|B) P(B) = P(A) P(B|A) is always true
◼A and B are independent iff
❑P(A,B) = P(A) P(B)
❑P(A) = P(A|B)
❑Prove that it’s also equivalent to P(B) = P(B|A)
◼Example
❑P(Toothache,Catch,Cavity,Weather) =
P(Toothache,Catch,Cavity) P(Weather)
❑P(Toothache,Catch,Cavity|Weather) =P(Toothache,Catch,Cavity)
❑P(Weather|Toothache,Catch,Cavity) = P(Weather)
Conditional Independence
5◼Independence is a strong assumption
❑e.g., toothache and catch are not independent (they are each
more likely if you have a cavity)
◼However, variables may be independent GIVEN other  
variables
◼Example:
❑P(toothache ∧ catch |cavity) = P(toothache|cavity) P(catch|cavity)
◼Interpretation1: If you have a cavity, the probability that the  
probe catches in your tooth is not affected by yourtoothache
◼Interpretation 2: If you have a cavity, the probability of you  
having a toothache is not affected by whether the probe  
catches it ornot
Conditional independence
6◼There are equivalent forms of conditional  
independence
◼A and B are conditionally independent given C iff
❑P(A, B | C) = P(A | C) P(B | C)
❑P(A | B, C) = P(A | C)
❑P(B | A, C) = P(B |C)
❑Proof: ?
◼Conditional independence is our most basic  
form of knowledge about stochastic  
environments
Cautions
7◼P(A,B) = P(A) P(B) means that it holds for all  
assignments of A andB
◼For example, if A and B are binary random variables, then
❑P(A=T,B=T) = P(A=T) P(B=T) (orP(a ∧     b)=P(a)P(b) )
❑P(A=T,B=F) = P(A=T) P(B=F) (orP(a ∧~b)=P(a)P(~b) )
❑P(A=F,B=T) = P(A=F) P(B=T) (orP(~a ∧     b)=P(~a)P(b) )
❑P(A=F,B=F) = P(A=F) P(B=F) (orP(~a ∧~b) = P(~a)P(~b) )
◼Is a much stronger claim than P(a ∧ b) = P(a) P(b)
❑It can also be mixed: P(A, b) = P(A) P(b)
❑The convention applies to all other equations
