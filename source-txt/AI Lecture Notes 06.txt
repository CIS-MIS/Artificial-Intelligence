1. INTRODUCTION TO UNSUPERVISED LEARNING
============================================================

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems can learn in different ways. One major approach is supervised learning, which uses labeled data to teach a model what the correct answers should be. Another approach is unsupervised learning, which allows a model to discover patterns and relationships in data without being given correct labels for every example.

Humans learn in both ways. In a classroom, a teacher may explain a concept and tell us whether our answers are correct. However, we also learn independently by observing the world around us.

Examples of human unsupervised learning:
- Recognizing that dogs and elephants are different animals by observing their characteristics.
- Discovering the rules of a sport by watching people play.
- Recognizing patterns in objects without someone explicitly naming or categorizing them.

AI researchers want computers to learn from observations in similar ways.

Definition:
Unsupervised learning is a type of machine learning in which a model analyzes data without being provided with labeled correct answers and attempts to discover useful patterns, structures, or relationships.

Why is unsupervised learning important?
- Large amounts of data are available without labels.
- Labeling data manually can be expensive and time-consuming.
- Models can discover patterns that people may not have identified beforehand.
- It can help AI systems learn useful representations of the world.
- It is useful for organizing data, grouping similar objects, and discovering hidden structures.

Examples of unsupervised learning applications:
- Grouping similar photographs.
- Discovering customer groups based on purchasing behavior.
- Organizing documents by topic.
- Identifying patterns in scientific data.
- Learning useful features from images, audio, or text.

============================================================
2. SUPERVISED LEARNING VS. UNSUPERVISED LEARNING
============================================================

SUPERVISED LEARNING

Supervised learning uses labeled examples. Each training example includes input data and a known target answer.

Example:
An AI receives photographs labeled "dog" or "cat." It learns to predict which category a new photograph belongs to.

The model compares its predictions with the correct labels during training and adjusts its parameters to reduce errors.

UNSUPERVISED LEARNING

Unsupervised learning uses data without supplied target labels. The model attempts to discover patterns or relationships within the data.

Example:
An AI receives hundreds of photographs of animals without labels. It may discover groups of photographs that contain visually similar animals.

The algorithm does not automatically know that one group represents dogs and another represents cats. It identifies patterns based on the features and learning method it uses.

KEY DIFFERENCE

- Supervised learning: Learns from examples with known target answers.
- Unsupervised learning: Discovers structure and patterns without supplied target answers.

Important clarification:
Unsupervised learning does not mean that a computer learns without any human involvement. Humans still design the algorithm, select or prepare the data, and decide what patterns the system should try to discover.

============================================================
3. PREDICTING THE WORLD
============================================================

One way to understand learning is to think about how humans predict what will happen next.

For example, if a video pauses while a tennis ball is moving, a person might predict where the ball will appear in the next frame.

Humans develop expectations about the world by observing many examples. AI researchers want to build systems that can learn useful patterns from large amounts of observations.

A model may learn relationships involving:
- The appearance of objects.
- The movement of objects.
- The relationship between different features.
- The structure of images.
- Patterns in language, sound, or other data.

The model's predictions can help it develop an internal understanding of the data.

Unlike supervised learning, where a teacher supplies the target answer, unsupervised learning can use the structure of the observed data itself to guide learning.

4. CLUSTERING: GROUPING SIMILAR OBJECTS
============================================================

One of the simplest ways to discover patterns in unlabeled data is clustering.

Definition:
Clustering is an unsupervised learning technique that groups data points according to their similarity.

The basic assumption is that some observations are more similar to each other than others.

Example: Grouping Flowers

Imagine four photographs of flowers with no labels.

We could organize the flowers using different characteristics:

A. Color
- Purple flowers.
- Yellow flowers.

B. Petal shape
- Flowers with rounded petals.
- Flowers with long, narrow, or vertically oriented petals.

C. Flower species
- Tulips.
- Sunflowers.
- Daisies.

Different features produce different groupings. The best grouping depends on the characteristics being examined and the purpose of the analysis.

Important concept:
Clustering does not automatically discover the one correct set of categories. The resulting groups depend on the features used, the algorithm, and the assumptions made about the data.

Clustering is useful when:
- Data does not have predefined labels.
- We want to discover natural groupings.
- We want to identify similarities among observations.
- We need to organize a large dataset into smaller groups.

Examples:
- Grouping customers with similar shopping habits.
- Grouping news articles with similar topics.
- Grouping images with similar visual characteristics.
- Grouping plants based on measured features.

5. CHOOSING FEATURES FOR A MACHINE LEARNING MODEL
============================================================

Before an AI model can analyze data, we must decide what information it should examine.

A feature is a measurable characteristic of an observation that a model uses as input.

For flowers, possible features include:
- Petal length.
- Petal width.
- Sepal length.
- Sepal width.
- Color.
- Shape.

Suppose all the flowers in a dataset are purple. Color may not be useful for distinguishing between them because the color does not vary.

However, the flowers may have different petal lengths and widths. These measurements could help the algorithm discover groups.

Example:
Imagine a graph with:
- The horizontal (X) axis representing petal width.
- The vertical (Y) axis representing petal length.

Each flower is represented by a point on the graph.

Flowers with similar petal measurements may appear close together, while flowers with different measurements may appear farther apart.

This transforms physical observations into numerical data that a computer can process.

Important vocabulary:

Observation:
A single example in a dataset. In the flower example, one observation represents one flower.

Feature:
A measurable property of an observation, such as petal width.

Feature vector:
A collection of numerical feature values describing one observation.

Dataset:
A collection of observations used for analysis or machine learning.

6. K-MEANS CLUSTERING
============================================================

K-means is an unsupervised learning algorithm that divides data into K clusters.

The letter K represents the number of clusters the algorithm is instructed to create.

For example:
- K = 2 means the algorithm creates two clusters.
- K = 3 means the algorithm creates three clusters.
- K = 5 means the algorithm creates five clusters.

The algorithm attempts to place similar data points into the same cluster by assigning them to the nearest cluster center.

A cluster center is called a centroid.

Definition:
A centroid is the mean position of the data points assigned to a particular cluster.

For numerical features, the centroid is calculated by finding the average value of each feature among the observations in that cluster.

For example, if three flowers have petal widths of 2, 4, and 6 units, their mean petal width is:

Mean = (2 + 4 + 6) / 3 = 4

The centroid calculation is performed separately for each feature.

K-means requires:
1. A dataset containing numerical features.
2. A method for measuring similarity or distance between observations.
3. A selected number of clusters, K.
4. A method for calculating the mean position of each cluster.

The standard K-means algorithm commonly uses Euclidean distance to measure how far a point is from a centroid.

============================================================
7. HOW THE K-MEANS ALGORITHM WORKS
============================================================

K-means repeatedly performs two main operations:
1. Assign data points to clusters.
2. Update the cluster centers.

These operations continue until the algorithm converges or reaches a stopping condition.

STEP 1: SELECT THE NUMBER OF CLUSTERS

Choose a value for K.

In the iris example, the assumption is that there are three groups, so K = 3.

The algorithm will attempt to create three clusters. It does not automatically know whether three is the correct number of natural groups in the dataset.

STEP 2: INITIALIZE THE CENTROIDS

The algorithm begins with initial cluster centers.

These centers may be selected randomly or through a more deliberate initialization method.

Because the starting centers may not represent the data well, the first cluster assignments can be inaccurate.

STEP 3: ASSIGN EACH DATA POINT

For every observation, calculate its distance from each centroid.

Assign the observation to the cluster with the nearest centroid.

For example:
- If a flower is closest to centroid 1, assign it to cluster 1.
- If it is closest to centroid 2, assign it to cluster 2.
- If it is closest to centroid 3, assign it to cluster 3.

The cluster assignments represent the algorithm's current estimate of which observations belong together.

STEP 4: RECALCULATE THE CENTROIDS

After assigning all observations, calculate the mean feature values of the data points in each cluster.

These new means become the updated centroids.

Because the cluster memberships may have changed, the centroids may move to new positions.

STEP 5: REPEAT THE PROCESS

Repeat the two main operations:

A. Assignment:
Assign each observation to its nearest centroid.

B. Update:
Recalculate the mean position of each cluster.

The process continues until the centroids stop changing significantly, the assignments stabilize, or another stopping condition is reached.

The algorithm may also stop after reaching a maximum number of iterations.

THE K-MEANS PROCESS IN SUMMARY

Choose K
    ↓
Initialize centroids
    ↓
Assign points to the nearest centroid
    ↓
Recalculate centroids
    ↓
Repeat assignment and recalculation
    ↓
Stop when the algorithm converges or meets its stopping condition

Important clarification:
K-means does not directly know which flowers belong to which biological species. It groups flowers according to their measured features and the distance-based rules of the algorithm.

8. THE IRIS FLOWER EXAMPLE
============================================================

The lecture uses a dataset containing measurements of iris flowers.

The goal is to discover groups among flowers without initially providing their species labels.

The model uses petal length and petal width as its features.

The algorithm is instructed to create three clusters:

K = 3

Initially, the cluster centers may be poorly positioned. Consequently, some flowers may be assigned to clusters that do not make much sense.

After recalculating the means, the centroids move.

The model then assigns each flower to its nearest updated centroid and recalculates the means again.

Repeated iterations gradually produce more stable groups.

The three iris species discussed in the example are:

1. Iris setosa.
2. Iris versicolor.
3. Iris virginica.

A gardening expert can examine representative flowers from each cluster and compare the groups with the known species.

In this example, the discovered clusters correspond well to the three iris species.

This provides evidence that the algorithm identified meaningful structure in the measurements.

However, the cluster numbers themselves have no biological meaning.

For example:
- Cluster 1 does not inherently mean Iris setosa.
- Cluster 2 does not inherently mean Iris versicolor.
- Cluster 3 does not inherently mean Iris virginica.

The numbers are simply identifiers assigned to the clusters. The relationship between each cluster and a real-world species must be determined separately.

Important lesson:
An unsupervised algorithm can discover groups without knowing their names. Humans can later interpret those groups and determine whether they are meaningful.

9. STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF K-MEANS
============================================================

STRENGTHS

- Relatively simple to understand and implement.
- Can organize large collections of numerical observations.
- Does not require labeled training examples.
- Can reveal patterns that are difficult to notice manually.
- Works well when the data contains reasonably distinct groups.

LIMITATIONS

1. The number of clusters must generally be chosen in advance.

   The algorithm does not automatically know the ideal value of K. Selecting a poor value can lead to unhelpful groupings.

2. Initial centroids affect the result.

   Different starting positions can lead to different final clusters.

3. Distance and feature selection matter.

   The algorithm groups points according to the features and distance measure used. If the features do not capture meaningful differences, the clusters may not be useful.

4. Features with large numerical scales can dominate.

   For example, if one feature ranges from 0 to 10 and another ranges from 0 to 10,000, the second feature may dominate distance calculations. Feature scaling may be necessary.

5. K-means works best with certain cluster shapes.

   It often performs well for compact groups but may struggle with irregularly shaped clusters, heavily overlapping groups, or data containing many outliers.

6. A cluster is not automatically a real-world category.

   The algorithm identifies mathematical groupings. Further analysis is needed to understand what those groups represent.

10. FROM SIMPLE DATA TO COMPLEX IMAGES
============================================================

K-means is relatively easy to visualize when the dataset has only two features.

For example, each flower can be represented using:
- Petal length.
- Petal width.

A graph can show the positions of the flowers and the locations of the cluster centers.

However, images are much more complicated.

An image contains many pixel values. A color image may represent each pixel using red, green, and blue (RGB) values.

A model analyzing photographs may need to process thousands or millions of numerical values across many images.

Possible image characteristics include:
- Color.
- Edges.
- Shapes.
- Textures.
- Objects.
- Spatial relationships between objects.

For example, an image of a dog and an image of a cat may have very different individual pixel values because of lighting, background, position, and color.

Simply averaging their pixels may not reveal the meaningful similarities between the animals.

This creates a major challenge:

How can a computer represent the important characteristics of an image instead of treating every pixel as an unrelated number?

The solution involves learning useful representations of data.

============================================================
11. REPRESENTATION LEARNING
============================================================

Definition:
Representation learning is a machine learning approach in which a model learns useful ways to represent data so that important patterns and relationships become easier to identify.

Instead of relying only on the original raw input values, a model learns internal features that summarize useful information.

For images, useful representations might capture:
- Edges and boundaries.
- Shapes.
- Textures.
- Parts of objects.
- Relationships among visual features.
- Higher-level patterns associated with complete objects.

For example, a model may learn that certain combinations of edges and shapes are useful for distinguishing a pizza from a flower.

A learned representation can help the model compare two images even when their individual pixels differ considerably.

Why is representation learning useful?

Raw data can be extremely complicated. Learning a useful representation can help a model identify important structure without requiring a person to manually define every relevant feature.

Representation learning is used in both supervised and unsupervised learning.

SUPERVISED REPRESENTATION LEARNING

A model learns useful internal features while being trained to predict known labels or answers.

Example:
A model learns visual features that help it classify photographs as cats or dogs.

UNSUPERVISED REPRESENTATION LEARNING

A model learns useful internal features from data without being given target labels for every example.

Example:
A model learns representations that capture similarities among photographs without being told which objects each photograph contains.

Important distinction:
Representation learning describes how a model learns useful features or internal descriptions of data. It is not limited to unsupervised learning.

============================================================
12. RECONSTRUCTION AS A WAY TO LEARN REPRESENTATIONS
============================================================

The lecture demonstrates representation learning through a simple human experiment.

A person looks at a picture for a few seconds and then attempts to draw it from memory.

The person cannot reproduce every pixel perfectly. Instead, they remember important characteristics of the image, such as:
- The main objects.
- Their shapes.
- Their relative positions.
- Important colors and textures.

The drawing is then compared with the original picture.

This illustrates two ideas:

1. Representation:
   The person internally retains selected information about the image.

2. Reconstruction:
   The person uses that internal information to recreate an approximation of the original image.

A machine learning model can perform a similar task.

For an AI system, reconstruction means generating an output that attempts to reproduce the original input data.

For an image, this could mean generating pixel values that resemble the original photograph.

A model may not need to preserve every detail perfectly to learn a useful internal representation. The goal is to capture important patterns in the input.

============================================================
13. AUTOENCODERS
============================================================

An autoencoder is a type of neural network commonly used to learn representations of data by attempting to reconstruct its input.

An autoencoder typically contains three conceptual parts:

1. Encoder.
2. Latent representation.
3. Decoder.

A. ENCODER

The encoder receives the original input and transforms it into an internal representation.

For example, the input may be an image containing many pixel values.

The encoder processes those values and learns a representation that captures useful information about the image.

B. LATENT REPRESENTATION

The latent representation is the internal encoded form of the input.

It may capture important information about the original image in a more compact or transformed form.

For example, the representation might encode information about the image's shapes, edges, textures, and object structure.

The exact information learned depends on the network architecture, training data, and learning objective.

C. DECODER

The decoder uses the latent representation to produce a reconstruction of the original input.

For an image autoencoder, the output is another image containing reconstructed pixel values.

AUTOENCODER STRUCTURE

Original image
    ↓
Encoder
    ↓
Latent representation
    ↓
Decoder
    ↓
Reconstructed image

The model compares the reconstructed image with the original image using a reconstruction loss.

The loss measures how different the reconstruction is from the input according to a selected mathematical measure.

During training, the model adjusts its weights and biases to reduce this loss.

As training progresses, the model may learn to reconstruct the inputs more accurately.

Important note:
An autoencoder is not necessarily required to produce a perfect reconstruction. It learns according to its architecture and training objective, and some information may be lost.

============================================================
14. HOW AN AUTOENCODER LEARNS
============================================================

An autoencoder learns by repeatedly processing inputs and measuring reconstruction errors.

The general process is:

STEP 1: INPUT

Provide an image to the model.

STEP 2: ENCODE

The encoder transforms the image into a learned internal representation.

STEP 3: DECODE

The decoder uses the representation to generate a reconstructed image.

STEP 4: CALCULATE RECONSTRUCTION LOSS

Compare the reconstructed image with the original image.

STEP 5: UPDATE PARAMETERS

Use an optimization method, commonly involving backpropagation and gradient-based optimization, to adjust the network's weights and biases.

STEP 6: REPEAT

Repeat the process across many training examples.

The objective is to reduce the reconstruction loss over time.

An autoencoder can learn without a separate human-provided label such as "pizza," "dog," or "flower." The original input itself serves as the target for reconstruction.

This is why autoencoders are commonly associated with unsupervised learning.

Technical clarification:
Autoencoders are often described as unsupervised learning models, although they can also be understood as using self-supervised learning because the target output is generated directly from the input data rather than supplied as an external label.

Imagine that an AI system must organize thousands of photographs.

- Animals.

These representations can then help a system compare photographs and identify meaningful similarities.

For example, images may be grouped based on representations that capture similar shapes or textures.

The important idea is that meaningful patterns often exist at a higher level than individual pixel values.

16. WHY UNSUPERVISED LEARNING IS DIFFICULT
============================================================

Although humans can learn many things simply by observing the world, building AI systems that do the same is challenging.

Humans have specialized biological systems for processing information involving:
- Vision.
- Language.
- Movement.
- Hearing.
- Spatial relationships.

These capabilities developed through billions of years of biological evolution.

AI systems do not automatically possess the same learning abilities.

Researchers must design models and learning objectives that help computers identify useful patterns.

Important challenges include:

1. Choosing the right learning objective.

   The system needs a mathematical goal that encourages it to learn useful patterns.

2. Selecting suitable data.

   The quality, diversity, and quantity of observations influence what the model can learn.

3. Learning useful representations.

   The model must capture meaningful structure rather than unimportant details.

4. Evaluating results.

   Without known labels, it can be difficult to determine whether the discovered patterns correspond to meaningful real-world categories.

5. Avoiding misleading patterns.

   A model may discover correlations that reflect background details, noise, or biases rather than the underlying concepts researchers care about.

6. Designing effective algorithms.

   Humans must still decide how the model processes information, updates its parameters, and evaluates its learning objective.

Unsupervised learning remains an important and active area of AI research.

17. THE ROLE OF HUMAN KNOWLEDGE
============================================================

In the iris example, the algorithm discovers three clusters without initially knowing the flower species.

A gardening expert later examines the flowers and identifies the species represented by the clusters.

This illustrates the difference between discovering patterns and interpreting them.

The algorithm can identify groups based on measurements, but it does not automatically understand what those groups mean in the real world.

A human expert can provide useful context by:
- Examining examples from each cluster.
- Comparing the groups with known categories.
- Determining whether the clusters make practical sense.
- Identifying limitations in the measurements.
- Explaining the real-world meaning of the results.

The use of an expert to interpret or evaluate clusters does not change the fact that the original clustering process was unsupervised.

The model did not need the expert's labels to produce the clusters. The expert helped determine what the discovered patterns represented.

21. FINAL SUMMARY
============================================================

Unsupervised learning allows artificial intelligence systems to discover patterns and relationships in data without relying on externally supplied target labels. One basic example is clustering, which groups observations according to their similarity. The K-means algorithm performs clustering by repeatedly assigning data points to their nearest centroid and updating each centroid using the mean of its assigned points.

For simple datasets, clustering can reveal useful patterns based on a small number of measurable features. For more complex data, such as photographs, a model may need to learn a representation that captures meaningful characteristics beyond individual pixel values.

Representation learning helps models develop useful internal descriptions of data. Autoencoders are one example: they encode an input into an internal representation and then attempt to reconstruct the original input. By reducing reconstruction errors, they can learn useful features without needing manually supplied category labels.

The central idea is that AI can learn from the structure of observed data. However, humans must still design the learning process and determine whether the patterns discovered by a model are meaningful.
